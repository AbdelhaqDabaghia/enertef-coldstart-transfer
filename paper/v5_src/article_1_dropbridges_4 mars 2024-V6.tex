\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

%%% ------------------------------------------ %%%
\medskip


\hspace{0.5cm}The Dalang--Morton--Willinger theorem asserts that, in a discrete-time model of a financial market, there is no arbitrage if and only if the price process is a martingale under an equivalent probability measure. This foundational result, often referred to as the \textit{First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing}, plays a central role in mathematical finance~\cite{Shiryaev1999}. Its apparently simple formulation has inspired numerous efforts to develop elementary proofs~\cite{ Dalang1990, DelbaenPreprint, Jacod1998, Rogers1994, Kabanov1994, Schachermayer1992, Stricker1990}. 

\hspace{0.5cm}Over the years, the theorem has been enriched by equivalent formulations that help distinguish it from the Harrison-Pliska theorem~\cite{Harrison1981}, which relies on the existence of a finite equivalent measure. Further generalizations have examined the representation of terminal portfolio gains through combinations of characteristic functions, but counterexamples show that such formulations may fail to eliminate arbitrage when convexity is absent~\cite{Yan1980}. Indeed, convexity appears to be a critical assumption for the equivalence between the absence of arbitrage and the existence of a martingale measure.

This note aims to provide a new, more accessible proof of the theorem. Our approach relies only on classical results, such as the Hahn–Banach and Halmos–Savage theorems~\cite{Halmos1949}, and may be more suitable for pedagogical purposes.

\hspace{0.5cm}Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathcal{P})$ be a probability space.  
We consider a finite discrete-time filtration\\ $\mathcal{F} = (\mathcal{F}_t)_{t=0,\dots,T}$, which represents the evolution of available information over time.
$\mathcal{P}$ is the set of all predictable d-dimensional processes.\\
Let $S = (S_t)_{t=0,\dots,T}$ be an adapted $d$-dimensional  process, where $S_t = (S_t^1, \dots, S_t^d)$ represents the prices of $d$ assets.
$H = (H_t)_{t=1,\dots,T}$ such that $H_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable for each $t$.

$$
\Delta S_t = S_t - S_{t-1}.
$$
We define the terminal gain by following a process $H \in \mathcal{P}$ up to time $T$ as:
\begin{equation}
H \cdot S_T := \sum_{t=1}^T H_t \cdot \Delta S_t.
\label{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo-1}
\end{equation}
 
And:
$$R_T:=\left\{\xi:  \xi=H \cdot S_T,  H \in \mathcal{P}\right\}$$




Put $A_T:=R_T-L_{+}^0$ where $L_{+}^0:\left\{\lambda I_A: \lambda\geq 0, A \in \mathcal{F}\right\}$ and let $\bar{A}_T$ be the closure of $A_T$ in probability.
 $L_{+}^0$ is the set of non-negative random variables ( $\mathcal{F}$-measurable). and $R_T-L_{+}^0$ can be interpreted as the set of hedgeable claims. 
    
\begin{theorem}
    

Assume that $A_T$ is convex. Then the following conditions are equivalent:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[(a)] $(R_T - L_+^0) \cap L_+^0 = \{0\}$;
    \item[(b)] $(R_T - L_+^0) \cap L_+^0 = \{0\}$ and $A_T = \bar{A}_T$;
    
    \item[(c)] $\bar{A}_T \cap L_+ = \{0\}$;
    \item[(d)] There is a probability $\tilde{P} \sim P$ with $\frac{d \tilde{P}}{d P} \in L^\infty$ such that $S$ is a $\tilde{P}$-martingale.
\end{itemize}

\end{theorem}

\hspace{0.5cm}\textsc{Remark 1.}
 If $A_T$ is convex (i.e. $0 \in A_T$ and for all $\lambda, \mu>0$ and $x, y \in A_T$ we have that $\left.\lambda x+\mu y \in A_T\right)$. 
Due to conditions (a), (d). Especially these conditions are equivalent to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item[(e)] $\quad R_T \cap L_0^{+}=\{0\}$;
    \item[(f)] $\left\{\eta \Delta S_t: \eta \in L^0\left(\mathcal{F}_{t-1}\right)\right\} \cap L_0^{+}=\{0\}$ for all $t \leq T$;
\end{itemize}    



Conditions (a) and (e) are called the no arbitrage (NA) property of the model whereas condition (f) is the NA property for one-step model.

The NA property for the class of all strategies (conditions (a),(e)) is equivalent to the NA property in the narrower class of bounded strategies $H$ see [3, Remark, p. 73 \cite{Kabanov2009}].\\
\hspace{0.5cm}\textsc{Remark 2.} Notice that if $A_T = \overline{R_T-L_{+}^0}$ is convex then :

$$
\overline{A_T} \cap L^0_+ = \{0\}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad 
\exists\, \tilde{P} \sim P,\ \frac{d\tilde{P}}{dP} \in L^\infty,\ \text{s.t. } S \text{ is a } \tilde{P}\text{-martingale}.
$$



And:
\\
$$
\exists\, \tilde{P} \sim P,\ \frac{d\tilde{P}}{dP} \in L^\infty,\ \text{s.t. } S \text{ is a } \tilde{P}\text{-martingale}.
\quad \Rightarrow \quad 
(R_T - L^0_+) \cap L^0_+ = \{0\}.
$$



\textsc{Proof:}
Let $\xi \in \overline{A_T} \cap L_+^0$, so $0 \leq \xi \leq H \cdot S_T$.  
Under the martingale measure,
$$
\tilde{E}[H_t \Delta S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0.
$$
By iterated conditioning:
$$
\tilde{E}[H \cdot S_T] = 0.
$$
Then $$\xi = 0$$

To prove the second implication in the remark, it remains to show $(\text{c}) \Rightarrow (\text{e})$.

Since $A_T \subseteq R_T - L_+^0$, it suffices to show: 
$$
\overline{R_T - L_+^0} \subseteq \bar{A}_T.
$$
We prove $R_T - L_+^0 \subseteq \bar{A}_T$.

Let $x = H S_T - r \in R_T - L_+^0$, where $r \in L_+^0$.  
Since $r = \lim r_n$ for simple functions $r_n$, we get  
$x = \lim(H S_T - r_n)$, and $H S_T - r_n \in A_T$ because $A_T$ is a convex cone.  
Hence, $x \in \bar{A}_T$, and Therefore:
$$
\overline{R_T - L_+^0} \subseteq \bar{A}_T.
$$

Now, we will use the space $\mathcal{L}_{+} := \left\{ \lambda I_A : \lambda > 0,\ A \in \mathcal{F} \right\}$, and the Dalang–Morton–Willinger theorem we have:

$$
\overline{R_T - L_+^0} \cap \mathcal{L}_+ = \{0\}.
$$
This implies:  
$$
\text{(*)} \quad (R_T - L_+^0) \cap \mathcal{L}_+ = \{0\}.
$$

\hspace{0.5cm}We show that (*) implies (a).  
Suppose (*) holds, and there exists  
$x \in (R_T - L_+^0) \cap L_+^0$, $x \neq 0$.  
Then $x = H S_T - h$, with $h \in L_+^0$, and for some $\varepsilon > 0$,  
$P(x \geq \varepsilon) > 0$.

Define $A := \{x \geq \varepsilon\}$. Since $x - \varepsilon I_A \geq 0$, we get:  
$$
\varepsilon I_A = H S_T - h - (x - \varepsilon I_A) \in R_T - L_+^0,
$$
contradicting (*). So, (*) $\Rightarrow$ (a).









 
\end{remarque}


\begin{Lemma}
    Let $\eta^n$ be a sequence of random vectors taking values in $\mathbb{R}^d$ such that for almost all $\omega \in \Omega$ we have:
\begin{equation}
\liminf_n \left\|\eta^n(\omega)\right\|_d < \infty.    
\end{equation}

\\Then there is a sequence of random vectors $\tilde{\eta}^n$ taking values in $\mathbb{R}^d$ satisfying the following conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item[(1)] $\tilde{\eta}^n$ converges pointwise to some random vector $\eta$ almost surely, where $\eta$ takes values in $\mathbb{R}^d$.
    \item[(2)] for almost all $\omega \in \Omega$, the sequence $\tilde{\eta}^n(\omega)$ is a convergent subsequence of the sequence $\eta^n(\omega)$.
\end{itemize}

\end{Lemma} 
\vspace{0.3cm}

\textsc{Proof:} Define the random variable $\eta_* := \liminf_n \left\| \eta^n \right\|_d.$ An increasing sequence of integer-valued random variables $\sigma_k$ such that the sequence $\eta^{\sigma_k}$ converges almost surely. Let $\sigma(0) := 0$ and define recursively:
$
\sigma(k) := \inf \left\{ n > \sigma(k-1) : \left| \left\| \eta^n \right\|_d - \eta_* \right| \leq \frac{1}{k} \right\}.
$
Now define the subsequence $\tilde{\eta}^n := \eta^{\sigma(n)}$. Notice that $\tilde{\eta}^n$ is a well-defined sequence of random vectors, and satisfies:
$$
\sup_n \left\| \tilde{\eta}^n \right\|_d < \infty \quad \text{a.s. on } \Omega.
$$

In particular, for the first coordinate we have: $\eta_*^1 := \liminf_n \eta^{n,1} < \infty.$ Define:
$$
\tau_1(0) := 0, \quad \tau_1(k) := \inf \left\{ n > \tau_1(k-1) : \left| \tilde{\eta}_n^1 - \eta_*^1 \right| \leq \frac{1}{k} \right\}, \quad k \geq 1.
$$

Repeat the same procedure for the second component using the sequence $\tilde{\eta}_{\tau_1(n)}$, and define $\tau_2(k)$ accordingly. Proceed inductively for all $d$ components.

Finally, define:
$
\tau_n := \tau_d \circ \cdots \circ \tau_1 \circ \sigma(n),
$
and set:
$
\tilde{\eta}^n := \eta^{\tau_n}.
$
Then, $\tilde{\eta}^n$ is a subsequence of $\eta^n$ that converges almost surely in $\mathbb{R}^d$, as required. \hfill $\square$


\begin{Lemma}\label{lem:important}
   Let $\eta^n \in L^0(\mathbb{R}^d)$ be such that $\eta := \liminf_n \|\eta^n\|_d < \infty$. Then there exists a sequence $\tilde{\eta}^n \in L^0(\mathbb{R}^d)$ such that for all $\omega$, the sequence $\tilde{\eta}^n(\omega)$ is a convergent subsequence of the sequence $\eta^n(\omega)$. 
\end{Lemma} 

\textsc{Proof:} Let $\sigma_0 := 0$. Define the random times
$
\sigma_k := \inf\left\{n > \sigma_{k-1} : \left| \|\eta^n\|_d - \eta \right| \leq \frac{1}{k} \right\}.
$
Then $\tilde{\eta}^n := \eta^{\sigma_n}$ is in $L^0(\mathbb{R}^d)$ and $\sup_n \|\tilde{\eta}^n\|_d < \infty$. Working further with the sequence $\tilde{\eta}^n$,\\ we construct, applying the above procedure to the first component, a sequence $\tilde{\eta}^{1,n}$ with convergent first component and such that for all $\omega$, the sequence $\tilde{\eta}^{1,n}(\omega)$ is a subsequence of $\tilde{\eta}^n(\omega)$.

Passing to the second component of the new sequence, we again apply the procedure and obtain a sequence $\tilde{\eta}^{2,n}$ with converging first two components, and so on. Iterating this construction through all $d$ components, we finally obtain a sequence $\hat{\eta}^n$ such that for all $\omega$, $\hat{\eta}^n(\omega)$ is a convergent subsequence of $\eta^n(\omega)$ with values in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and the convergence is almost sure. \hfill $\square$



\hspace{1cm}\begin{Lemma}
Let $K \supseteq -L_+$ be a closed convex cone in $L^1$ such that $K \cap L_+ = \{0\}$. Then there exists a probability measure $\widetilde{P} \sim P$ such that $\frac{d\widetilde{P}}{dP} \in L^\infty$ and

$\tilde{E} \xi \leq 0$ for all $\xi \in K$.
\end{Lemma}












\textit{Proof:}
By the Hahn-Banach theorem for any $x \in L_{+}^1, x \neq 0$, there is $z_x \in L^{\infty}$ such that $E z_x \xi<E z_x x$ for all $\xi \in K$. Since $K$ is a cone, we obtain $Ez_x \xi \leq 0$ for all $\xi \in K$. 
define the set $A := \{z_x < 0\}$ and suppose $P(A) > 0$. Consider the sequence $\xi_n := -\lambda_n \mathbf{1}_A$, with $\lambda_n \to \infty$, so that $\xi_n \in K$ by the cone property Then :
$$
Ez_x \xi_n = -\lambda_n Ez_x \mathbf{1}_A \to \infty,
$$
which contradicts $Ez_x \xi \leq 0$. Hence, $z_x \leq 1$ almost surely. and finaly $0 \leq z_x \leq 1$.
The Halmos-Savage theorem asserts that the family of measures $\left\{z_x P\right\}$ contains a countable equivalent subfamily $\left\{z_{x_i} P, i \in \mathbf{N}\right\}$ (i.e., both vanish on the same sets).


Define
$
\rho := \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} 2^{-i} z_{x_i} \in L^\infty, \quad \text{and} \quad \bar{x} := \mathbf{I}_{\{\rho = 0\}}.
$
Then for each $i$, we have $Ez_{x_i} \bar{x} = 0$, which implies $\bar{x} = 0$ a.s. because otherwise $\bar{x} \in L_+$ and $Ez_{x_i} \bar{x} > 0$.

Thus, $\rho > 0$ a.s. and we can define the measure $\widetilde{P}$ via:
$$
\frac{d\widetilde{P}}{dP} = c \rho, \quad \text{with } c = \frac{1}{E\rho},
$$
so that $\widetilde{P}$ is a probability measure equivalent to $P$. For any $\xi \in K$:
$$
E_{\widetilde{P}}\xi = Ec \rho \xi = c \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} 2^{-i} Ez_{x_i} \xi \leq 0,
$$
since each $Ez_{x_i} \xi \leq 0$ by construction. This completes the proof.


















\hspace{0.5cm}\textsc{Remark 2.} The Halmos-Savage theorem, we consider the larger family $\{y P\}$ where $y$ are convex combinations of $z_x$. Then ess sup $I_{\{y>0\}}$ can be attained on an increasing sequence of $I_{\left\{y_k>0\right\}}$. Clearly, $\left\{y_k P\right\}$ is a countable equivalent subfamily of $\{y P\}$ and it is a convex envelope of a countable family $\left\{z_{x_i} P\right\}$ we are looking for.


Let $\{z_x\}_{x \in X}$ be a family of positive measurable functions on a measurable space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$, with respect to a reference measure P, where X is uncountable.

We define:
$$
Z := \{ z_x : x \in X \}, \quad Y := \left\{ y = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i z_{x_i} : n \in \mathbb{N}, \lambda_i \geq 0, \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 1, x_i \in X \right\}.
$$


Then Y is the finite convex hull of Z. Consider the family of measures $\mathcal{P} := \{ y P : y \in Y \}.$

Define:
$$
A := \bigcup_{y \in Y} \{ \omega \in \Omega : y(\omega) > 0 \} = \operatorname{ess\,sup}_{y \in Y} I_{\{ y > 0 \}},
$$

which represents the union of the supports of the densities y \in Y.

Since the essential supremum over indicator functions can be attained by an increasing \\ sequence, there exists a sequence $\{y_k\}_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \subset Y$ such that :

\begin{equation}
I_{\{y_1 > 0\}} \le I_{\{y_2 > 0\}} \le \cdots \nearrow \operatorname{ess\,sup}_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} I_{\{ y > 0 \}} .
\end{equation}


Hence,
$$
\bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \{ y_k > 0 \} = A 
$$
And: 
$$
y_k = \sum_{i=1}^{n_k} \lambda_i^{(k)} z_{x_i^{(k)}}, \quad \text{with } x_i^{(k)} \in X.
$$

Define:
$
X_0 := \bigcup_{k \in \mathbb{N}} \{ x_i^{(k)} : 1 \le i \le n_k \},
$
which is a countable subset of $X$, and hence each $y_k$ is a convex combination of \{ z_x : x \in X_0 \}.

Set:
$
Z_0 := \{ z_x : x \in X_0 \}, \quad Y_0 := \operatorname{conv}(Z_0),
$
where $\operatorname{conv}(\cdot)$ denotes the convex hull with finite combinations.

\end{rem}

\hspace{0.5cm}(a) $\Rightarrow$ (b) 
By Lemma~\ref{lem:important}
, we can show that $A_T$ is closed using simple recursion, even without assuming $\mathcal{F}_0$ is trivial.

Let’s take $T = 1$. Assume $H_1^n \Delta S_1 - r^n \to \zeta$ a.s., where $H_1^n$ is $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable and $r^n \in L_+^0$.  
We want $\zeta = H_1 \Delta S_1 - r$ for some $H_1$ ($\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable) and $r \in L_+^0$.

Each $H_1^n$ is a column vector. Stack them as a matrix:

$$
\mathbf{H}_1:=\left[\begin{array}{cccccc}
H_1^{11} & H_1^{21} & \ldots & \ldots & H_1^{n 1} & \ldots \\
H_1^{12} & H_1^{22} & \ldots & \ldots & H_1^{n 2} & \ldots \\
\ldots & \ldots & \ldots & \ldots & \ldots & \ldots \\
H_1^{1 d} & H_1^{2 d} & \ldots & \ldots & H_1^{n d} & \ldots
\end{array}\right] .
$$

If the matrix is zero, we’re done.  
Assume the result holds when the matrix has at least $m$ zero rows (pointwise). We now show it holds when there are $m - 1$ zero rows.

We just need to find $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable $\tilde{H}_1^k \to \tilde{H}_1$ a.s. and $\tilde{r}^k \in L_+^0$, such that:
$$
\tilde{H}_1^k \Delta S_1 - \tilde{r}^k \to \zeta \quad \text{a.s.}
$$

Let $\Omega_i \in \mathcal{F}_0$ form a finite partition of $\Omega$.  
We can work on each $\Omega_i$ separately as a separate measure space.

Let $\underline{H}_1 := \liminf |H_1^n|$.  
On $\Omega_1 := \{\underline{H}_1 < \infty\}$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:important}, we can choose $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable $\tilde{H}_1^k$ converging a.s. to a limit, and define $\tilde{r}^k$ accordingly.  
If $\Omega_1$ has full measure, we are done.

On $\Omega_2 := \{\underline{H}_1 = \infty\}$, set  
$G_1^n := H_1^n / |H_1^n|$, $h_1^n := r_1^n / |H_1^n|$.  
Then $G_1^n \Delta S_1 - h_1^n \to 0$ a.s.  
By Lemma~\ref{lem:important}, we find\\
$$\tilde{G}_1^k \to \tilde{G}_1$$ And $$\tilde{G}_1 \Delta S_1 = \tilde{h}_1 \geq 0$$  
By (a), this gives $$\tilde{G}_1 \Delta S_1 = 0$$

Since $\tilde{G}_1(\omega) \neq 0$, partition $\Omega_2$ into $\Omega_2^i \in \mathcal{F}_0$ such that $\tilde{G}_1^i \neq 0$ on $\Omega_2^i$.  
Define $\bar{H}_1^n := H_1^n - \beta^n \tilde{G}_1$, with  
$\beta^n := H_1^{ni} / \tilde{G}_1^i$ on $\Omega_2^i$.  
Then $\bar{H}_1^n \Delta S_1 = H_1^n \Delta S_1$ on $\Omega_2$.  
Now the modified matrix $\overline{\mathbf{H}}_1$ has at least $m$ zero rows.  
Apply the induction hypothesis.

\bigskip

\hspace{0.5cm}To continue the time induction, assume the result holds for $(T-1)$-step models. 
\\
Let $\sum_{t=1}^T H_t^n \Delta S_t - r^n \to \zeta$ a.s., with $H_t^n$ $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable, and $r^n \in L_+^0$.

As in the one-step case, use $\mathbf{H}_1$.  
On $\Omega_1$, take $\mathcal{F}_0$-measurable $\tau_k$ such that $H^k := H_1^{\tau_k} \to H_1$.  
Then $\sum_{t=2}^T H_t^{\tau_k} \Delta S_t - r^{\tau_k} \to \zeta - H_1 \Delta S_1$,  
reducing to the $(T-1)$-step case.

On $\Omega_2$, use the same induction in $m$ (zero rows), applying the same operations to all matrices $\mathbf{H}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{H}_T$.



\hspace{0.5cm}(b) $\Rightarrow$ (c) Notice that $A_T \subseteq R_T-L_{+}^0$ and $L_{+} \subseteq L_{+}^0$.

\hspace{0.5cm}(c) $\Rightarrow$ (d)
For any random variable $\eta$, there exists an equivalent probability $P'$ with bounded density such that $\eta \in L^1(P')$ e.g., take $P' = C e^{-|\eta|} P$.

Property (c) (as well as (a) and (b)) is preserved under equivalent change of measure.  
So we can assume all $S_t$ are integrable.

Define $A_T^1 := \bar{A}_T \cap L^1$, which is convex and closed in $L^1$.  
Since $A_T^1 \cap L_+^1 = \{0\}$, (by Theorem 2.1.4 ,~\cite{Kabanov2009}), there exists $\tilde{P} \sim P$ with bounded density such that:
$
\tilde{E}[\xi] \leq 0 \quad \text{for all } \xi \in A_T^1.
$

In particular, this holds for $\xi = \pm H_t \Delta S_t$, where $H_t$ is bounded and $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable.  
Hence,
$$
\tilde{E}[\Delta S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0.
$$
\hspace{0.5cm}(d) $\Rightarrow$ (a) 
Let $\xi \in A_T \cap L_+^0$, so $0 \leq \xi \leq H \cdot S_T$.  
Under a martingale measure,\\
$
\tilde{E}[H_t \Delta S_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}] = 0.
$
Then, by iterated expectations,
$
\tilde{E}[H \cdot S_T] = 0.
$
Thus, $\xi = 0$.

Let us consider a capillary bridge, whose liquid phase is denoted $\Omega_l$  of volume $V$, between two solid substrates $\Omega_s$ in contact with the liquid on the portion $\omega_{sl}$ of its boundary.
The other part of the boundary of the solid $\Omega_s$, which is  surrounded by  the gas phase, is noted $\omega_{sg}$. Obviously,  we have  $\partial \Omega_s =  \omega_{sl} \cup  \omega_{sg} $. The capillary bridge  (fluid phase $\Omega_l$) is in contact with the gas on the part $\omega_{lg}$ of its boundary, such that $\partial \Omega_l =  \omega_{lg} \cup  \omega_{sl} $. \\
\medskip
The thermodynamical system $\Omega$ considered here consists of the liquid phase  $\Omega_l$, of the  solid substrates $\Omega_s$ and of their interfaces $\omega_{lg}$, $\omega_{sl}$ and $\omega_{sg}$. 
 At thermodynamical equilibrium (no phase change, constant chemical potentials and temperature), the relevant state function of the system is its Grand potential\footnote{Referred to as Grand Canonical potential as well.}  $E_{\Omega}$ \cite{DeGennes_2004}. 
%
\medskip
In the absence of body force acting on the system, we consider that the  Grand potential of the system only depends on surface  densities of free energy, $\galg$, $\gasl$, $\gasg$, respectively, assumed to be constant    \textcolor{red}{ [il faudrait citer une ref sur la forme classique de $E_{\Omega}$ sans $\gaslg$]} and also on and a line energy $\gaslg$ assumed to be constant as well:
%
\begin{equation}
	 E_{\Omega} = \galg  \Alg \  + \gasl  \Asl  +  \gasg    \Asg \pm \gaslg   \Lslg  
	\label{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo-2}
\end{equation}
where $\ \Alg$ denotes  the  liquid-gas interface area, $\Asl$  the solid-liquid interface area,  $\Asg$  the solid-gas interface area and $ \Lslg$ is  the length of the contact line. 
The $\pm$ sign distinguishes the cases where the contact line is advancing on a dry solid substrate before reaching its equilibrium (positive sign), from the case where the contact line is receding on the substrate (negative sign). Note that in the definition (\ref{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo-2})  of the free energy of the system,  we propose to add a supplementary term modeling the line energy $ \pm \gaslg   \Lslg  
$ which is less classical\footnote{\textcolor{red}{Its existence have been discussed in  [citer qq refs]. } } and enables to model the hysteresis of the contact angle (advancing/receding) which is observed experimentally  \textcolor{red}{[citer qq refs]. } Without adding this line energy contribution, the energy minimization of the system would lead to the  classical Young equation which does not take into account this hysteresis effect (see section \ref{sec:GYL}).  

%
\medskip
As we have obviously
$\Asl  +  \Asg = A_s $ where $A_s$ denoted the total surface of the solid substates,  the free energy variation of the system $\delta E_{\Omega}$  may be easily deduced from Eq. (\ref{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo-2}) provided the solid substrates are rigid and therefore $\delta  A_s = 0$:
\begin{equation}
	 \delta   E_{\Omega} = \galg  \delta    + \Alg \   (\gasl - \gasg) \delta    \Asl   \pm \gaslg  \delta    \Lslg  
	\label{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo-3}
\end{equation}
%%
%%
%\medskip
For the considered assumptions the first law of thermodynamics reads in adiabatic conditions:
\begin{equation}
	\delta E_{\Omega} - W_{F_{\textrm{ext/sys}}} = 0
	\label{eq_1er_Princ_Thermo}
\end{equation}
where $W_{F_{\textrm{ext/sys}}}$ is the related mechanical work performed by the resultant of external forces acting on the system $\Omega$. The latter may be decomposed into, on one hand the pressure forces acting on the fluid phase ${\Omega_l} $ and on the second hand the external forces acting on the solid substrates  ${\Omega_s} $.  \\
%%
%%
The elementary  resultant  pressure forces acting on the fluid phase writes\footnote{They  are exerted on the  liquid-gas interface $\omega_{lg} $ and on  the solid-gas interface  $\omega_{lg} $ (as the solid in rigid, we have a transmission of pressure force  due to the gas through it).} :
$$d {F_{\textrm{ext}/{\Omega_l}}}
 = \Delta P \ dS  \  n_{\Omega_l}$$ 
where $n_{\Omega_l}$ denotes the unit external normal to  $\Omega_l$  and $\Delta P=P_{\text{liquid}} - P_{\text{gas}}$ the capillary pressure (pressure difference between inside and outside the capillary bridge). 
%
%
\medskip
The mechanical work of the external pressure force then may be written as:
\begin{equation}
	W_{F_{\textrm{ext}/{\Omega_l}}}
	= \int_{\partial\Omega}   \Delta P \ n_\Omega.\delta u \  dS
		\label{work-Fp}
\end{equation}
where $ \delta u$ denoted the  displacement corresponding to the variation $\delta$. As the capillary pressure is constant, we get\footnote{It can be proved in a rigorous and general way that 
$ \displaystyle{ \int_{\omega_{lg} }       n_{lg}.  \delta u  dS  +  \  \int_{\omega_{ls} }    n_{ls}. \delta u   dS  =  \delta V}$ whatever the displacement  $\delta u$ is. }:
\begin{equation}
	W_{F_{\textrm{ext}/{\Omega_l}}}
 =\Delta P \left(   \int_{\omega_{lg} }       n_{lg}.  \delta u  dS  +  \  \int_{\omega_{ls} }     n_{ls}. \delta u  dS  \right)   = \Delta P \  \delta V
		\label{work-Fp2}
\end{equation}
 where $\delta V$ is the volume variation of the liquid bridge and  $n_{lg}$ (respectively $n_{ls}$) the unit outer normal to $\omega_{lg} $ (respectively to $\omega_{ls}$). 
%
%
\medskip
Therefore, the first law of thermodynamics reduces to the following equation: 
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:energie_plan}
 \galg  \delta  \Alg \  + (\gasl - \gasg)  \delta \Asl \pm \gaslg  \delta  \Lslg  - \Delta P \  \delta V -   W_{F_{\textrm{ext}/{\Omega_s}}}
= 0
\end{equation}
where $W_{F_{\textrm{ext}/{\Omega_s}}}$ denoted the work of external forces exerted on the solid substrates $\Omega_s$.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}

In this work, we proposed a general framework to deduce both Young-Laplace and Young equations for an axisymmetrical capillary doublet %
from the minimization of total energy of the system. %
Young equation naturally appears as a boundary condition associated to Young-Laplace equation. %
%%
A supplementary term, linked to a contact line energy is added in the whole energy of the system and enabled to obtain a generalized Young equation modelling the hysteresis of contact angle, %
observed experimentally (advancing/receding contact angles). %
%%
Moreover, this approach leads to a general expression of the associated capillary force expressed at the contact line. %
This expression of the capillary force, \textcolor{red}{which is not influenced by the line energy, } corresponds to those obtained from the first integral of Young-Laplace equation and is equivalent to the classical %
``gorge method''. %%Gras 2011, Lian 1993.
However, it may be more convenient to compute it at the contact line. %
%%
The case of a sessile drop is treated with the same approach as a limit case of %
a \emph{just broken} capillary bridge between two parallel plates. %
%%
A generalized Young equation containing a line energy contribution is obtained as well.
