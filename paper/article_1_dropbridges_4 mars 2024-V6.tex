\title{Representation Beats Regularization in Energy Transfer}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Abdelhaq Dabaghia\IEEEauthorrefmark{1}}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\IEEEauthorrefmark{1}EnerTEF, Service~1 --- Energy Community Forecasting\\
Email: (corresponding author)}}

\begin{abstract}
We study whether a data-scarce target site can bootstrap an electric-vehicle
(EV) charging-demand forecaster by transferring weights and/or Fisher-importance
estimates from a mature production model, and then keep adapting without
forgetting the source domain. Unlike most continual-learning (CL) studies that
rely on synthetic task splits, we validate on a \emph{real, published,
independent} target: the UK \emph{Electric Nation} smart-charging trial, with
features made column-for-column identical to a deployed Luxembourg CNN--LSTM.
We implement a single parameterised protocol covering six conditions
(from-scratch, warm-start, Elastic Weight Consolidation~(EWC) with source or
target Fisher, and replay) and evaluate plasticity (target error) against
stability (source retention) over multiple data budgets and three seeds.
Three findings emerge. (i)~\textbf{Warm-start transfer helps substantially}
(${\sim}40\%$ lower normalised RMSE than training from scratch at every budget).
(ii)~\textbf{Replay dominates the stability--plasticity trade-off}; EWC adds
nothing over a plain warm-start fine-tune. (iii)~In a controlled ablation --- the
paper's core contribution --- we show the transferred Fisher's apparent harm is a
\emph{scale artifact} (the source Fisher is ${\sim}211\times$ larger); once scales
are equalised, the Fisher's \emph{origin} does not matter, and \emph{EWC itself}
provides no benefit for this transfer. The full CL taxonomy
(EWC, MAS, LwF, DER++, A-GEM), an improved-EWC bench, and a learning-rate/epoch
sweep all fail to beat plain warm-start on plasticity or simple replay on
retention. We then \textbf{replicate the entire protocol on a second, physically
distinct task} --- PV generation forecasting (Luxembourg $\rightarrow$ a real
published household in Konstanz, Germany) --- and obtain the same verdict.
A mechanistic analysis explains \emph{why} replay wins in cross-regime transfer:
the Fisher-important directions coincide with the useful target gradients
($\mathrm{cosine}{=}0.82$), so regularising toward the source weights fights
exactly the updates the target needs, whereas replay works by direct source
coverage. Finally, and most importantly, we show the true bottleneck is the
\textbf{output representation, not the algorithm}: two domain-native reformulations --- per-unit
normalisation (power engineering) and the clear-sky index (solar engineering) ---
cut transfer error by ${\sim}33\%$ (EV) and ${\sim}32\times$ (PV) and collapse the
source/target Fisher-scale mismatch from $211\times$ to ${\sim}2\times$. A
\textbf{controlled representation ablation} further shows that, among
representations, the best choice depends on how much domain structure it encodes:
a generic learned per-instance normalisation (RevIN) beats the thin per-unit
representation on EV ($p{=}0.008$), whereas the richer clear-sky index beats RevIN
on PV ($p{=}0.008$). Across
nine real European countries, the transfer gap grows with domain distance
($\rho{=}0.88$, $p{=}0.004$) --- but this is largely mediated by the target's
intrinsic predictability rather than a pure distance penalty. We characterise each domain shift (dominated by a
$6\times$ demand-scale gap for EV, $50\times$ for PV) and release a fully
reproducible, GPU-accelerated pipeline.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Continual learning, transfer learning, catastrophic forgetting, elastic weight

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
%=====================================================================
Data-driven load forecasters deployed in energy communities operate in
\emph{non-stationary} environments: consumption patterns drift, new assets come
online, and new sites must be served with little or no local history. Two
learning paradigms address this. \emph{Transfer learning} reuses a model trained
on a data-rich source to bootstrap a data-scarce target~\cite{PanYang2010}.
\emph{Continual learning} (CL) lets a deployed model keep adapting to new data
while resisting \emph{catastrophic forgetting}~\cite{McCloskey1989,French1999}
of previously acquired knowledge. Our production system (Service~1 of an Horizon
Europe project) already combines both: an EV CNN--LSTM demand forecaster for a
Luxembourg energy community is nightly fine-tuned with Elastic Weight
Consolidation~(EWC)~\cite{Kirkpatrick2017} and automatically promoted.

This paper asks a deployment-relevant question: \emph{can a new, low-data target
site bootstrap its forecaster by transferring weights and/or Fisher importance
from the mature Luxembourg model, and then continue to adapt via EWC without
losing this transferred knowledge?} Crucially, we validate on a \emph{real,
published, independent} target --- the UK \emph{Electric Nation} home
smart-charging trial --- rather than on a synthetic split of our own data, which
is the norm in the CL literature.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{single parameterised protocol} instantiating six conditions
(from-scratch, warm-start, EWC with source/target Fisher, and replay) on an
industrial architecture, with exact feature/scaler fidelity to a deployed model
(verified to machine precision).
\item A rigorous \emph{stability--plasticity} evaluation on a real cross-country
transfer, over data budgets $N\!\in\!\{1,7,30,60,90\}$ days and three seeds.
\item A \emph{Fisher-transferability ablation with a fairness control}: we show
the source and target Fisher differ ${\sim}211\times$ in scale, and that once
equalised, the Fisher's origin has no measurable effect --- correcting a
tempting but wrong ``transferred Fisher is harmful'' conclusion.
\item An honest \emph{negative result}, hardened by an improved-EWC bench (true
empirical Fisher, per-layer normalisation, EWC$+$replay) and a learning-rate/epoch
baseline: \textbf{simple warm-start$+$replay beats every EWC variant tested}.
\item A precise \emph{domain-shift characterisation} and a fully reproducible,
GPU-accelerated pipeline.
\item A \emph{controlled representation ablation} against the per-instance
normalisation SOTA (RevIN), showing the best representation depends on the
\emph{richness} of the domain knowledge it encodes: RevIN beats thin per-unit on
EV, but rich clear-sky beats RevIN on PV (both Wilcoxon $p{=}0.008$, $8$ seeds).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{Continual learning} methods fall into three families~\cite{DeLange2021}:
\emph{regularisation-based} methods penalise changes to parameters deemed
important for past tasks --- EWC~\cite{Kirkpatrick2017} via a diagonal Fisher,
Synaptic Intelligence~\cite{Zenke2017}, Memory Aware Synapses~\cite{Aljundi2018},
and Learning without Forgetting~\cite{LiHoiem2017}; \emph{replay/rehearsal}
methods re-expose the model to stored or generated past samples ---
iCaRL~\cite{Rebuffi2017}, Experience Replay~\cite{Rolnick2019}, and Dark
Experience Replay~(DER++)~\cite{Buzzega2020}; and \emph{parameter-isolation}
methods dedicate distinct capacity per task. Online/consolidated EWC variants
such as Progress~\&~Compress~\cite{Schwarz2018} maintain a running Fisher.

\textbf{CL and transfer for energy forecasting.}
Continual learning has been increasingly applied to load and building-energy
forecasting to cope with concept drift without storing large
histories~\cite{ContLoadPartsCleaning2025,BuildingEnergyCL2023,OODLoadCovid2023}.
Recent work benchmarks CL under out-of-distribution shifts (e.g.\ COVID-19 load
regimes)~\cite{OODLoadCovid2023} and for real-world non-stationary power
systems~\cite{PracticalCLEnergy2026}, and a first survey targets CL for time
series~\cite{CLTimeSeriesSurvey2024}. \textbf{Transfer learning for EV/charging
demand} is motivated by data scarcity at new sites: knowledge is transferred from
data-rich to data-limited regions~\cite{EVTransferGaps2025}, sometimes combined
with clustering to handle behavioural heterogeneity~\cite{EVScarceCluster2025}.

\textbf{Representation and normalisation.} The two reformulations we exploit are
individually well known. The \emph{per-unit} system --- normalising power by a
rated base --- is textbook in power engineering~\cite{Glover}, and capacity- or
maximum-value normalisation into $[0,1]$ is standard practice in load-forecasting
transfer, where target series are rescaled to a common range or made
building-agnostic before fine-tuning~\cite{TLnorm}. In solar forecasting, the
\emph{clear-sky index} (power or irradiance divided by a clear-sky model) is the
routine way to de-seasonalise the diurnal/annual trend and obtain a near-stationary
target in $[0,1]$; it is widely reported to improve accuracy regardless of the
model~\cite{Inman2013,Yang2018}. A complementary, model-agnostic line learns
\emph{per-instance} normalisation --- reversible instance normalisation
(RevIN)~\cite{RevIN2022} standardises each window by its own statistics and
reverses this on the output, with adaptive variants modelling evolving statistics
(Dish-TS, SAN, FAN)~\cite{DishTS2023,SAN2023,FAN2024}; whether RevIN's affine layer
is even needed is debated~\cite{RevINrole2026}. We use RevIN as the strong modern
baseline against which our domain representations are compared.
Our contribution is \emph{not} these normalisations
but the finding that, in \emph{cross-regime transfer}, the choice of output
representation --- not the continual-learning algorithm --- is the dominant lever:
we quantify that per-unit collapses the source/target Fisher-scale mismatch from
$211\times$ to ${\sim}2\times$ and that these reformulations cut transfer error by
${\sim}33\%$ (EV) and ${\sim}32\times$ (PV), and we connect this directly to why
weight-anchoring regularisers fail (Sec.~\ref{sec:mech}).

\textbf{Gap.} Most CL/transfer forecasting studies use synthetic splits or a
single method, and rarely (a)~validate transfer on an \emph{independent published}
target, (b)~jointly measure plasticity \emph{and} source retention, (c)~control
for the \emph{scale} of the importance matrix when comparing Fisher variants, or
(d)~ask whether the \emph{representation}, rather than the algorithm, is what
limits transfer. We address all four, and report a rigorous negative result on EWC
alongside a positive representation result and a mechanistic explanation.

\section{Problem Setup and Data}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Source and target domains}
The \emph{source} $\thesource$ is the Luxembourg production domain: aggregated EV
charging power (two chargers, EMOB1$+$EMOB2) at $15$-min resolution over the
period both chargers are active (2024-09-10 to 2026-01-18; $47{,}524$ steps),
with the earliest $38{,}883$ steps used to fit the production feature scalers and
the last $90$ days held out. The \emph{target} $\thetarget$ is the UK
\emph{Electric Nation} trial: ${\sim}72$ aggregated home chargers, converted from
Amps to kW assuming $230$\,V single-phase, at $15$-min resolution over
2017-06-28 to 2018-12-16 ($51{,}467$ steps). Weather is drawn from the Open-Meteo
archive: for the target, a single representative point (Bristol) stands in for the
whole trial service area; for the source, Copal~Dudelange
($49.71^\circ$N, $6.49^\circ$E).

\subsection{Features}
Each step carries the $21$ sequential features used by the production model, in a
fixed order: \texttt{ev\_kw}; lags $\{1,4,96,672\}$; rolling means over
$\{1,6,24\}$\,h; six weather variables (shortwave/direct/diffuse radiation, cloud
cover, temperature, wind speed); and seven cyclic time encodings (time-of-day,
day-of-year, day-of-week sines/cosines, weekend flag). The ``next-step''
exogenous vector is the same list minus \texttt{ev\_kw} ($20$ features). We reuse
the \emph{frozen} production \texttt{MinMaxScaler}s for all conditions; our
reconstruction reproduces every scaler min/max to machine precision, and the
train-row count reproduces the frozen $n_{\mathrm{samples}}=38{,}883$ exactly,
guarding against silent feature/scaler drift.

\subsection{Domain shift}
Table~\ref{tab:domain} reports the source-vs-target shift. It is dominated by the
demand variable itself: a $6\times$ scale gap and the largest normalised
$1$-Wasserstein distance ($0.62$), together with wind speed ($0.63$, coastal
Bristol vs.\ inland Dudelange). Radiation, cloud and temperature transfer well
(normalised Wasserstein $0.05$--$0.23$; standardised weather maximum mean
discrepancy MMD$^2=0.034$~\cite{Gretton2012}).

\section{Method}
%=====================================================================
\subsection{Forecaster architecture}
We reproduce the deployed two-branch EV CNN--LSTM exactly
(Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}): a sequential branch takes the $(672,21)$ lookback through
two causal \texttt{Conv1D}$(64,k{=}5)$ layers and an \texttt{LSTM}$(64)$; a
next-step branch takes the $(20)$ exogenous vector through a
\texttt{Dense}$(64)$; the two are concatenated and passed through
\texttt{Dense}$(128)\!\rightarrow\!\texttt{Dense}(1)$ with dropout. The head is
linear; non-negativity is enforced post-hoc. Loss is Huber ($\delta{=}0.5$),
optimiser Adam. A construction-vs-deployment fidelity check confirms our builder
reproduces the promoted model's outputs to $\max|\Delta|=0$.

\subsection{Cold-start transfer protocol}
Let $\theta^{\star}$ be the transferred production weights. We define six
conditions, all sharing the frozen scalers and evaluated on a fixed final
$14$-day target holdout:
\emph{B0} from scratch (random init);
\emph{B1} warm-start $+$ EWC with a Fisher estimated on the \emph{source};
\emph{B2/B3} warm-start, plain fine-tune (no EWC);
\emph{B4} warm-start $+$ EWC with a Fisher \emph{re-estimated on the target};
\emph{B5} warm-start $+$ replay of source samples mixed into each batch.
For the ``cold-start'' regime, only the first $N$ days of the target stream are
available for training.

\subsection{EWC objective and Fisher estimators}
The EWC objective reuses the production form
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EWC}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}(\theta)
+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i F_{ii}\,(\theta_i-\theta^{\star}_i)^2 ,
\end{equation}
with $\lambda$ the regularisation strength. The production Fisher is an
output-sensitivity proxy over $B$ mini-batches of $M{=}500$ sampled points,
\begin{equation}
F_{ii}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_b\!\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_i}
\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathrm{batch}_b|}\sum_j \hat{y}_j^2\Big)\right]^{2}.
\label{eq:proxyfisher}
\end{equation}
As a control we also use the \emph{true empirical} diagonal Fisher, the mean
squared gradient of the per-example Huber loss w.r.t.\ each parameter,
\begin{equation}
\tilde F_{ii}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m}\Big(\frac{\partial \ell(\theta;x_m,y_m)}
{\partial \theta_i}\Big)^{2}.
\label{eq:empfisher}
\end{equation}
For replay, the loss adds $\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}}(\theta;\text{source
batch})$.

\subsection{Fisher-scale fairness control}
Comparing B1 (source Fisher) and B4 (target Fisher) at a shared $\lambda$ is
confounded if the two Fishers differ in scale: the effective regularisation would
differ. We therefore also \emph{normalise} each Fisher to unit mean before
applying $\lambda$ (globally, or per layer), which makes $\lambda$'s effective
strength comparable across Fisher origins, and we sweep $\lambda$ for both.

\subsection{Metrics}
Plasticity is the target normalised RMSE,
$\nrmse=\mathrm{RMSE}/\overline{y}$ (kW, un-scaled) on the target holdout.
Stability is the \emph{source-retention} $\nrmse$: the same adapted model
evaluated on a fixed Luxembourg holdout, without further training. We also report
\emph{data-to-threshold}: the smallest $N$ at which a condition first drops below
a chosen $\nrmse$ gate.

\section{Experiments and Results}
%=====================================================================
\textbf{Setup.} All runs use Adam ($10^{-4}$), $10$ epochs, batch $64$, and three
seeds; the pipeline runs on a single NVIDIA RTX~5070 (TensorFlow~$2.21$ /
CUDA~$12.9$ under WSL2). A pre-flight sanity check confirms the reloaded
production model yields a non-degenerate MAE ($18.6$~kW on the $7$-day source
holdout) far below the frozen baseline ($42.0$~kW), validating the scaler and
architecture path before any transfer.

\subsection{RQ1 --- Cold-start transfer benefit}
Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1} and Table~\ref{tab:rq1} show that warm-start (B3/B4) reduces
target $\nrmse$ by ${\sim}40\%$ versus from-scratch (B0) at \emph{every} budget,
with small, well-separated variance. From-scratch is unstable across $N$; the
warm-started models plateau near $0.52$ from $N{=}30$ on. Adding EWC (B4) is
statistically indistinguishable from a plain warm-start (B3).

\subsection{RQ2 --- Stability--plasticity trade-off}
At a data-rich checkpoint ($N{=}30$), we measure both target error and source
retention (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq2}, Table~\ref{tab:rq2}; $8$ seeds). \textbf{Replay
(B5) wins the trade-off}: it achieves by far the best source retention ($0.43$
vs.\ $0.56$ for warm-start). Paired Wilcoxon tests over the $8$ seeds make the
claims precise: replay improves retention significantly ($p{=}0.008$) at a
significant target cost ($p{=}0.008$), whereas \textbf{EWC (B4) is
\emph{statistically indistinguishable} from plain warm-start (B3) on target error}
($p{=}0.31$) --- EWC buys no plasticity. From-scratch (B0) is worst on both axes
(it never saw the source).

\subsection{RQ3 --- Fisher transferability, with a fairness control}
Naively, B1 (source Fisher) looks much worse than B4 (target Fisher) on both axes
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2}). But the source Fisher's raw scale is
\emph{$211\times$ larger} than the target Fisher's (sum/mean ratio; $30\times$ in
$L_2$) --- a direct consequence of the $6\times$ demand-scale gap propagating
through Eq.~\eqref{eq:proxyfisher}. At a shared $\lambda$, B1 therefore
over-regularises ${\sim}211\times$ harder. Normalising each Fisher to unit mean
and sweeping $\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq3}, Table~\ref{tab:rq3}) removes the
confound: B1 and B4 become \emph{comparable} (B1 even marginally better on
target), and \emph{neither} beats the plain warm-start. \textbf{The Fisher's
origin does not matter once scales are equalised; EWC itself provides no benefit
for this transfer.} This corrects the tempting but wrong conclusion that the
transferred Fisher is intrinsically harmful.

\subsection{Improved-EWC bench and simple baselines}
To pre-empt the objection that a better-designed EWC would win, we bench three
honest variants against the B3/B5 baselines (Fig.~\ref{fig:bench},
Table~\ref{tab:bench}): \emph{V1} EWC with the true empirical Fisher
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:empfisher}); \emph{V2} per-layer normalised Fisher; \emph{V3} an
EWC$+$replay hybrid. \textbf{None beats warm-start on plasticity or replay on
retention}; the hybrid even degrades pure replay. Finally, a simple
learning-rate/epoch sweep (a trivial ``adapt less, forget less'' knob) is
\emph{strictly dominated}: reducing LR or epochs worsens both axes, and its best
retention ($0.56$) never approaches replay's ($0.40$). Thus replay's retention
advantage is real and not reproducible by simple under-regularisation or by EWC.

\section{Second Task: PV Generation (Luxembourg $\rightarrow$ Konstanz)}
%=====================================================================
To test whether our conclusions generalise beyond EV demand, we replicate the
\emph{entire} protocol on a second, physically distinct task --- PV generation
forecasting --- with the deployed PV LSTM (LOOKBACK $96$, $11$ features, $148{,}161$
parameters; our builder reproduces it to $\max|\Delta|{=}0$). The source is the
Luxembourg community PV (\texttt{ECC\_master}, matched to the frozen scaler
exactly: $71{,}043$ train steps); the \emph{target} is a real, published,
\emph{independent} site: household \texttt{DE\_KN\_residential3} of the OPSD
household dataset~\cite{OPSD2020} (Konstanz, Germany), a cumulative-energy meter
we convert to power ($90{,}217$ $15$-min steps, 2016--2018), with weather
re-fetched from Open-Meteo. This is a \emph{near}-climate transfer (both temperate
northern Europe) but with an even larger target-scale gap
($50\times$: a $751$\,kW community array vs.\ a ${\sim}15$\,kW rooftop). The
domain shift is again dominated by the generation variable
($W_{\mathrm{norm}}{=}0.75$) while weather transfers well
(MMD$^2{=}0.027$)~\cite{Gretton2012}.

Results \textbf{corroborate the EV findings} (Fig.~\ref{fig:pvrq1},
Table~\ref{tab:pv}). \emph{(i)}~Warm-start is essential: it cuts target RMSE by
${\sim}97\%$ vs.\ from-scratch (which, with random init on scarce data and the
$751$\,kW source scaler, produces wildly out-of-range predictions).
\emph{(ii)}~EWC (B1/B4) is statistically indistinguishable from a plain warm-start
on plasticity and does not beat replay on retention; here B1 (source Fisher) is
even marginally \emph{better} than B4 on retention, reinforcing that \emph{the
Fisher's origin does not matter}. \emph{(iii)}~Replay again yields by far the best
source retention, at a clearer plasticity cost on this task. Two tasks of very
different physical nature thus reach the same verdict: \emph{warm-start$+$replay
wins; EWC adds nothing}. (For PV we report RMSE in kW, since mean-normalised nRMSE
is inflated by night-time zeros --- a metric caveat rarely acknowledged.)

\subsection{Domain distance and the transfer gap: across locations}
Does the transfer gap grow with domain distance? We test this at two scopes.
\emph{Within one location}, six OPSD Konstanz PV systems (capacities
$7.6$--$15$\,kW) show robust warm-start generalisation ($13$--$43\times$ over
from-scratch) but no significant distance--gap trend --- expected, since a single
location holds the weather distance nearly constant. \emph{Across locations}, we
therefore transfer the (per-unit) Luxembourg PV source to \emph{nine} countries'
real national solar generation (OPSD, 2018--2019: DE, CZ, FR, DK, IT, GR, ES, PT,
plus two unreliable series dropped), so the weather distance now varies from
Central Europe to the sunny South.

Across these nine targets the transfer gap \textbf{does grow with domain distance}:
the generation-shape Wasserstein distance correlates with warm-start error at
Spearman $\rho{=}0.88$ ($p{=}0.004$; weather-MMD $\rho{=}0.69$, $p{=}0.06$)
(Fig.~\ref{fig:multi}). \emph{However}, this is largely \textbf{mediated by the
target's intrinsic predictability}: a model-free climatology baseline explains
$94\%$ of the warm-start-error variance ($r{=}0.94$, $p{=}0.001$) and is itself
collinear with distance (distant southern targets have more variable generation).
Controlling for climatology, no clean distance-specific residual survives at this
sample size. Thus domain distance is a useful \emph{predictor} of the transfer
gap --- a relation rarely established on real, geographically diverse targets ---
but it acts \emph{mostly through intrinsic target difficulty} rather than a pure
distance penalty. This is, to our knowledge, the first such decomposition for
energy-forecasting transfer, and a caution against reading distance as a direct
transfer cost.

\section{Representation Matters: Two Domain-Native Reformulations}
%=====================================================================
Our uniformly negative CL results raise a sharper question: if no algorithm
helps, what actually \emph{limits} transfer? We find the answer is the
\textbf{output representation}, not the continual-learning method --- and that two
reformulations borrowed from energy and solar engineering substantially improve
transfer while leaving the CL conclusion intact. Because these change the target
representation, the frozen production weights no longer apply; we therefore train
a source model \emph{from scratch} in each representation and transfer it (B0 vs.\
B3), a self-contained test of the representation hypothesis.

\subsection{Per-unit normalisation (EV)}
In the power-engineering \emph{per-unit} system, each site's power is divided by
its rated capacity, yielding dimensionless, cross-site-comparable quantities.
Re-training the EV source model in per-unit and transferring to the UK
(Table~\ref{tab:reform}) has two effects. First, the source/target Fisher ratio
\textbf{collapses from $211\times$ to ${\sim}2\times$}: the mismatch that
motivated our RQ3 fairness control was itself a scaling artefact, not an intrinsic
property. Second, warm-start transfer error \textbf{drops from $\nrmse{=}0.53$ to
$0.35{\pm}0.02$} (a ${\sim}33\%$ reduction, 5 seeds), while the
warm-start$\gg$from-scratch gap persists. Crucially, the gain is \emph{not} an
artefact of re-training the source: applied directly to the \textbf{deployed
production model} (no retraining --- the target is simply rescaled to its own
per-unit base), per-unit still cuts transfer error from $0.51$ to
$0.39{\pm}0.04$ (${\sim}22\%$, 3 seeds), confirming a genuine transfer improvement. Part of the ``high absolute error'' we (and a critical reviewer) noted
was thus an artefact of scaling target power through source-fitted min--max
scalers.

\subsection{Clear-sky index (PV)}
Solar forecasting rarely predicts raw power; it predicts the \emph{clear-sky
index} $\mathrm{csi}=\text{power}/\text{clear-sky envelope}$, a near-stationary,
location-agnostic target. We estimate the envelope empirically (a high power
quantile per day-of-year bin and time-of-day, fit on training data only) and
forecast csi. Re-training the PV source model in csi space and transferring to
Konstanz \textbf{reduces target RMSE from ${\sim}6.85$\,kW to $0.22{\pm}0.001$\,kW}
(5 seeds, ${\sim}32\times$)
(Table~\ref{tab:reform}) and removes the night-time-zero pathology that inflated
mean-normalised nRMSE. The physical constraint $\text{power}=\mathrm{csi}\times
\text{envelope}\ge 0$ also rules out the non-physical predictions that plagued
from-scratch models.

algorithm but a change of representation imported from the application domain.

\subsection{Representation ablation: which property matters?}
\label{sec:ablation}
The two reformulations above beat the raw pipeline, but \emph{why} --- is it merely
scale-invariance, or the specific domain knowledge? To isolate the property that
matters, we run a controlled ablation: fixing the architecture, the algorithm
(warm-start B3 vs.\ from-scratch B0), the budget ($N{=}30$) and the exogenous
inputs, we vary \emph{only} how the power channel and target are represented, and
compare three families against the modern per-instance-normalisation SOTA. We
compare, all evaluated back in kW over $8$ seeds: \emph{static global} scalers
(z-score, robust median/IQR, log), the \emph{learned per-instance} RevIN (affine-free
form, standardising each window by its lookback mean/std and de-normalising the
prediction likewise), and the \emph{domain-informed} representations
(per-unit for EV; clear-sky for PV). Min--max is excluded (source-fit min--max
places the target in a tiny sub-range and is uniformly worst, a known pathology).

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} gives two results. \emph{(1) Representation is the dominant
lever}: within a task the best and worst representations differ by up to $8\times$
in warm-start error, with architecture and algorithm fixed --- larger than any CL
effect in Secs.~\ref{sec:mech}. Global affine centring (z-score, robust) is even
\emph{actively harmful} on PV, where the day/night bimodal distribution makes
de-normalised predictions diverge. \emph{(2) The winner depends on domain-knowledge
richness}: on EV, \textbf{RevIN wins} ($\nrmse\,0.116{\pm}.003$), beating the thin
per-unit representation ($0.273{\pm}.010$) and all global scalers (paired Wilcoxon,
$8$ seeds, RevIN vs.\ per-unit $p{=}0.008$). On PV, \textbf{clear-sky wins}
($0.409{\pm}.013$), beating RevIN ($0.645{\pm}.019$) and log ($0.661{\pm}.065$)
(clear-sky vs.\ RevIN $p{=}0.008$). The contrast is the \emph{amount of structure}
injected: per-unit encodes a single capacity scalar and loses to a generic learned
per-instance normaliser; clear-sky encodes a full astronomical envelope and beats
it. In short, a learned reversible per-instance normalisation is a very strong
\emph{default} --- neutralising cross-site scale \emph{and} local non-stationarity
generically --- and is surpassed only by a domain representation rich enough to
carry more relevant structure.

\subsection{A scale-decomposition model of the Fisher mismatch}
\label{sec:scale-model}
The $211\times\!\to\!2\times$ collapse invites a theoretical question: \emph{under
what conditions must} per-unit normalisation reduce the source--target importance
mismatch? We deliberately do \emph{not} claim the unconditional inequality
$M(\Omega^{\mathrm{norm}})\le M(\Omega^{\mathrm{raw}})$: normalisation changes the
optimisation trajectory, the learned representation, and the local gradients, so
for two independently trained networks a chosen mismatch statistic \emph{can}
increase. A useful result must therefore be \emph{conditional}. The key is to
separate a \emph{nuisance scale} discrepancy (different site capacities / output
magnitudes) from a \emph{structural} discrepancy (genuinely different dynamics,
weather, behaviour) --- normalisation is designed to remove the former, not the
latter.

Let $\Omega_d\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}$ be the diagonal importance vector at site $d$
and $S(\Omega_d)=\tfrac1p\sum_j\Omega_{d,j}>0$ its mean. Working in logarithms
makes scale factors additive; we use the \emph{symmetric} log-mismatch
\begin{equation}
M(\Omega_s,\Omega_t)=\bigl|\log S(\Omega_s)-\log S(\Omega_t)\bigr|,
\label{eq:logmismatch}
\end{equation}
preferred over a raw ratio because $M(\Omega_s,\Omega_t)=M(\Omega_t,\Omega_s)$.
We assume only a \emph{multiplicative} scale model (the exponent is left
unspecified --- a controlled fit suggesting $c_d\propto b_d^{4}$ is reported as
supplementary evidence, not required here):
\begin{equation}
S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{raw}})=c_d\,S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{struct}}),\qquad c_d>0,
\label{eq:scalemodel}
\end{equation}
with $c_d$ a site-dependent nuisance factor and $S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{struct}})$ the
scale-free component.

\paragraph{Idealisation.} If normalisation exactly removes the nuisance factor,
$S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{norm}})=S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{struct}})$, then writing
$A_{s,t}=\log(c_s/c_t)$ (nuisance discrepancy) and
$R_{s,t}=\log\!\bigl(S(\Omega_s^{\mathrm{struct}})/S(\Omega_t^{\mathrm{struct}})\bigr)$
(structural residual), Eq.~\eqref{eq:scalemodel} gives
$M_{\mathrm{raw}}=|A_{s,t}+R_{s,t}|$ and $M_{\mathrm{norm}}=|R_{s,t}|$.

\begin{proposition}[Scale-aligned mismatch reduction]
\label{prop:scale}
If the nuisance and structural discrepancies share sign,
$A_{s,t}\,R_{s,t}\ge 0$, then normalisation cannot increase the mismatch:
$M_{\mathrm{norm}}\le M_{\mathrm{raw}}$; if additionally $A_{s,t}\neq 0$ the
reduction is strict.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
When $A_{s,t}R_{s,t}\ge 0$ the two terms share sign (or one is zero), so
$|A_{s,t}+R_{s,t}|=|A_{s,t}|+|R_{s,t}|\ge|R_{s,t}|=M_{\mathrm{norm}}$; strictness
follows when $|A_{s,t}|>0$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Realistic version.} For \emph{separately} trained raw and normalised
models Eq.~\eqref{eq:scalemodel} holds only up to a residual
$\varepsilon_d$ (retraining, representation drift, estimation noise):
$\log S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{raw}})=\log c_d+\log S(\Omega_d^{\mathrm{norm}})+\varepsilon_d$,
so $M_{\mathrm{raw}}=|A_{s,t}+R_{s,t}+E_{s,t}|$ with $E_{s,t}=\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_t$.
A robust sufficient condition for reduction is then
\begin{equation}
A_{s,t}\,R_{s,t}\ge 0 \quad\text{and}\quad |E_{s,t}|<|A_{s,t}|,
\label{eq:robustcond}
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the nuisance scale \emph{reinforces} rather than cancels the structural
mismatch, and retraining-induced drift is smaller than the removed scale effect.

\paragraph{Empirical test of the condition.} We verify
Eq.~\eqref{eq:robustcond} directly, with \emph{no retraining}, by estimating
$S(\Omega)$ (mean diagonal output-sensitivity Fisher) on the \textbf{deployed
production model} for the LU source and the UK target, in the raw and per-unit
representations ($N{=}30$, 3 seeds). We measure
$A_{s,t}=+4.40{\pm}0.12$ and $R_{s,t}=+0.40{\pm}0.06$: both are positive (the
sign condition $A_{s,t}R_{s,t}>0$ holds on \emph{every} seed), and the residual
$|E_{s,t}|$ is contained within the reported spread, well below $|A_{s,t}|$. The
mismatch collapses from $M_{\mathrm{raw}}{=}4.80$ (a ${\sim}122\times$ ratio) to
$M_{\mathrm{norm}}{=}0.40$ (${\sim}1.5\times$), an $81\times$ reduction --- the
deployed-model counterpart of the $211\times\!\to\!2\times$ figure obtained when
the source is retrained (Table~\ref{tab:reform}). Two facts matter for honesty:
the reduction is a \emph{consequence of a verified sufficient condition}, not an
assumption; and $R_{s,t}>0$ shows a genuine residual structural difference
survives normalisation --- per-unit removes the scale nuisance, not all cross-site
mismatch. We make no claim that the removed factor $c_d$ is \emph{exactly} physical
capacity or exactly $b_d^{4}$; only that a dominant, sign-aligned scale component
is removed.

\section{Mechanistic Analysis: Why Replay Beats Regularization}
\label{sec:mech}
%=====================================================================
Beyond \emph{that} replay wins, we explain \emph{why}, with three probes at the
production weights $\theta^{\star}$ (EV, $N{=}30$; Table~\ref{tab:mech}).

\noindent\textbf{(b) Fisher-important directions coincide with the useful target
gradients.} The source Fisher importance $F$ and the target-loss gradient
magnitude $|g|$ are strongly aligned: $\mathrm{cosine}(F,|g|){=}0.82$,
Spearman $\rho{=}0.61$, and \textbf{$62\%$ of the target-gradient energy lies in
the top-decile Fisher directions} (vs.\ $10\%$ if unrelated). Because source and
target share the \emph{task} but differ in \emph{regime}, the parameters that
matter for the source are exactly those the target must move.

\noindent\textbf{(a) EWC redirects motion away from the useful directions.} Over
fine-tuning, EWC (B1) moves the weights a similar total distance to a plain
fine-tune (relative displacement $0.019$ vs.\ $0.020$) yet its target error barely
falls ($0.80\!\to\!0.75$ vs.\ $0.76\!\to\!0.47$ for plain), and its source
retention is \emph{no better} ($0.55$ vs.\ $0.52$). EWC does not freeze motion --
it steers it \emph{out of} the high-Fisher (i.e.\ target-useful) directions, so the
motion buys neither plasticity nor stability.

\noindent\textbf{(c) Replay works by coverage, not magic.} Sweeping the replay
buffer size, source retention only improves once the buffer covers the source
distribution: nRMSE $0.50$ at $50$ samples (no gain over warm-start) $\to 0.39$ at
${\ge}10^3$ samples, then plateaus. Replay's advantage is direct re-exposure and
costs ${\sim}10^3$ stored source samples --- a concrete, honest memory budget.

\noindent Together: in cross-regime transfer, regularising toward $\theta^{\star}$
fights exactly the updates the target needs, while replay supplies the source
objective directly --- provided it covers the source.

%=====================================================================
\section{Discussion}
%=====================================================================
Why does EWC fail here while replay succeeds? EWC anchors parameters toward
$\theta^{\star}$ weighted by source importance. When the target demand
distribution differs by $6\times$ in scale, (i)~the source Fisher is dominated by
that scale, so at matched $\lambda$ it over-constrains, and (ii)~even a
well-scaled or target-estimated Fisher pulls the model toward a solution that no
longer fits the target, hurting plasticity without buying retention. Replay, by
contrast, keeps a small, direct signal of the source objective in every update,
which we find is the only mechanism that measurably preserves source performance.
The practical implication for deployed community forecasters is clear:
\emph{for cross-site cold-start, prefer weight transfer plus a small replay buffer
over EWC regularisation}.

%=====================================================================
\section{Limitations}
%=====================================================================
We state these plainly. (1)~\emph{Limited diversity}: we cover two tasks and,
for PV, six single-location plus nine cross-country target sites; the ``EWC does
not help'' claim is empirical for these transfers. The distance$\rightarrow$gap
relation is significant across countries but largely mediated by intrinsic target
predictability; isolating a pure distance penalty would need more sites/seeds to
break the distance--predictability collinearity. (2)~\emph{Weather approximations}: the UK site
uses a single representative point, and the source weather was re-fetched from
Open-Meteo (min/max reproduce the scaler exactly, but individual values
approximate the original snapshot). (3)~The UK data reflects a \emph{smart-charging
trial intervention}, not ``natural'' demand. (4)~\emph{Absolute error is high}
(${\sim}0.52$ $\nrmse$ even warm-started): the large demand shift caps transfer
quality; the benefit is \emph{relative} to from-scratch. (5)~\emph{Three seeds}
(run-to-run CV up to ${\sim}35\%$) suffice to separate the main effects but $5$--$10$
are advisable for strong claims.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%=====================================================================
On real cross-country cold-start transfers for \emph{two} physically distinct
tasks --- EV demand and PV generation --- weight transfer strongly helps, replay
best preserves the source domain, and \emph{no} EWC variant --- including the true
empirical Fisher, per-layer normalisation, and an EWC$+$replay hybrid --- beats a
simple warm-start$+$replay. A scale-fairness control shows the transferred
Fisher's apparent harm is an artifact, not an intrinsic property. A first
nine-country study shows the transfer gap grows with domain distance
($\rho{=}0.88$) but is largely mediated by intrinsic target predictability.
Future work adds geographically diverse targets to establish that relation, and
evaluates replay-buffer sizing under longer continual streams.
